Radio range

Seven ways
to reach each other.

How a Siflo phone reaches another one: over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, the internet, a LoRa radio and, later, satellite. For each link, the estimated range, the phones that can use it and what gets through.

The ranges are estimated by calculation, not yet measured in the field. The scale is logarithmic: from one mark to the next, the distance is multiplied by ten. The first six links are in the app, including the alert beacon (Android only) and the LoRa radio (an add-on); satellite is in preparation.

Reading these figures

A range is not a promise:
the terrain decides.

One hop, then the next

Each range covers a single hop, from one phone to the next. Every phone in the group relays the others' messages, up to 12 hops: the chain reaches much further than a single hop.

Long range takes two

It only works between two compatible phones. On iPhone, Siflo stays on standard Bluetooth, but a nearby Android can relay for it over long range.

Terrain matters more than the link

Rock blocks everything: in a cave, messages go from phone to phone, in line of sight. Wet foliage divides the range by two or three, by our estimates. Nothing gets through under water.

Estimates, not yet measurements

They are calculated from the transmit power, the receivers' sensitivity and the losses due to the body and the terrain. We will measure them in the field and update this page.

The alert beacon is one-way

The distant phone receives the alert but cannot answer along this path: there is no acknowledgement, and the alert is repeated every two seconds. The alert beacon has been in the Android app since version 0.5; its range is yet to be measured in the field.

Satellite, in preparation

Some recent Android phones send short messages by satellite, depending on the carrier; on iPhone, Apple keeps satellite for its own features. We are working on sending Siflo’s SOS and signals by satellite; nothing is available yet.

LoRa radio, an add-on

A small off-the-shelf radio, Meshtastic-compatible (Heltec Mesh Node T1, RAK WisMesh Tag), paired with the phone over Bluetooth. It carries messages, signals and SOS, not photos or voice notes. It only steps in for members that Bluetooth and the internet no longer reach.

LoRa radios as relays

A radio left on a high point or at a cave entrance relays the group’s messages with no phone attached; you set it up from Siflo’s settings. Radios relay up to 7 hops; beyond that, a phone with a radio carries the chain on.

End to end

Group and private messages are encrypted (ChaCha20-Poly1305) and signed (Ed25519) on the sending phone, and only their recipients can open them. An SOS sent to every phone nearby (position included) and public messages are meant for anyone: they are signed, not encrypted.

Keys that change

In a private conversation, from the first answer on, each message has its own key, erased once used (the Double Ratchet, Signal’s mechanism): a key stolen later cannot read past messages. In a group, the key changes every day and whenever a member is removed. A stolen key cannot read messages more than two days old, but it can read the following ones until the next time a member is removed.

Verifiable identities

Each phone makes its own keys: no account. To be sure you are talking to the right person, scan their code face to face, or compare a safety code during a call.

Over LoRa radio

A LoRa frame carries 233 bytes at most: an 8-byte tag, the message id, the time, then the text, encrypted the same way; a long message spans several frames. The tag is computed afresh for each frame, so that frames of one group cannot be linked. A phone that relays over the radio a message it got over Bluetooth adds its author’s signature: it cannot write in their place.

Who sees what

A member of the group
The whole message, and its author, checked by their signature.
A stranger’s phone that relays it
Only the header: the time, the size, and a tag that changes every day and reveals neither the group nor the author.
Our internet relays
The same, plus the IP address of the phone that drops off or picks up the packet. They keep it for 48 hours without being able to open it.
Someone listening in on LoRa traffic
The number of the radio that transmits, the time and size of each frame: enough to recognise a radio from one day to the next, but not the text, the author or the group.
Guardia, which publishes Siflo
Nothing more than our relay servers: no account, no address book, no content.

Two exceptions, both about LoRa radios: the backup channel, which you turn on yourself, shows a conversation’s messages in plain text on the screens of the group’s radios, to read them without a phone (the app shows an open padlock); an SOS also appears there in plain text, unless you opt out when sending it.

How we checked it

We described the protocol in mathematical models. Two proof tools (Tamarin, ProVerif) and a tool that explores the possible courses of events in a small group (TLA+, 3 or 4 phones) pit them against an attacker who controls the whole network, relays included, can steal a key at any moment, or used to be a member or an admin of the group. An admin still in the role is assumed to act in good faith.

    How far these checks go

    The models cover the contact exchange, the start of private conversations and groups (keys, admins, members); for admins and members, tests also check these rules on the app’s own code. Outside the models: packet routing and parsing, the radio, media, traffic analysis, and the rest of a private conversation, which relies on the Double Ratchet’s published proofs. These results hold from version 0.20.0 on.
