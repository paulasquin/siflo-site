The problem

Thousands of phones, one cell tower.

On concert night, thousands of phones share the same towers: messages get stuck, calls fail, and not everyone has a radio. When someone collapses in the front row, the first-aid post has no idea.

With Siflo

The message, though, gets round the whole group.

Julie, volunteering by the stage, types “Someone collapsed, front row, left side”. Marc’s phone, in the middle of the crowd, passes the message on to Léa and Tom at the first-aid post, without going through the tower.

No network · 01

Every phone passes the word on.

Over Bluetooth, every Siflo phone passes the messages on to the next: about 25 m in a pocket, about a hundred metres in hand with a clear line of sight, up to 300 m between two Android phones with long-range Bluetooth, and up to 12 hops. On Android, nearby Wi-Fi speeds up photos and voice notes. No cell tower needed: every phone in the group becomes one.

No network · 02

Two subgroups too far apart? The internet links them.

If one phone on each side has signal, messages go through our relay servers, which keep them for 48 hours without being able to read them. These servers are independent: if one goes down, another takes over.

No network · 03

Messages wait for whoever lags behind.

The teammate who stayed behind for a photo gets everything as soon as they come back near another phone. A signal that arrives several minutes late shows up without ringing: no need to stop for an expired STOP.

SOS

Hold the SOS button for two seconds.

A five-second countdown lets you cancel, then your SOS, with your location if you allow it, reaches every Siflo phone in range, including those of other groups. It also goes to the group of your choice, over the internet when someone has signal. It goes out again every minute, like the six whistle blasts of the mountain distress signal. Siflo can also prepare a text message to the numbers saved in the group. The SOS does not call emergency services: if you have signal, dial 112. It is the one Siflo button we hope you never try.

In the field

When voice and whistle
are not enough.

Situations described by manufacturers, federations and operators, with what Siflo changes. The sources are under each example, for the sceptics.

Canyoning · releasable rigging

Asking for more rope from inside the waterfall

The first person rappels on a rope that is deliberately too short, and has to ask the teammate at the anchor for slack. Under the waterfall, voices do not carry, and the whistle code had better have been agreed beforehand.

With Siflo: “Release the rope” reaches only the phone of the teammate at the anchor, with the name of whoever is asking.

Source: Petzl, “Securing the first person on rappel” (in French)

Canyoning · teammate in trouble

Warning the person at the anchor about a problem they cannot see

Bulges, overhangs, rappels in the flow: a stuck teammate can be invisible from above. Hand signs and whistles only work if everyone learnt the same codes. And remembers them.

With Siflo: you spell it out to the whole group, “Léa stuck under the waterfall, release”. Nothing to remember.

Source: French canyoning instructor handbook (in French)

Caving · wet pitch

Getting through to those at the top of the pitch

At the bottom of a wet pitch, a caver thinks the rope is damaged. Passed on by shouting, the message gets garbled and ends up triggering a rescue call-out, over a leaf caught on the rope. Word of mouth has its limits, especially underground.

With Siflo: with a teammate halfway down to link the phones, “STOP, don’t come down” and then the explanation arrive word for word, with the sender’s name.

Source: ULSA incident report, Yorkshire, 2025

Climbing · at the crag

Not mixing up the calls

Out of sight, the climber calls that they are safe, or that they want to be lowered. One misheard call, or one shouted by the party next door, is enough to send someone to the ground.

With Siflo: create “Safe” and “Lower me” signals. They reach your belayer’s phone only, never those of the party next door.

Source: FFME safety letter no. 8 (in French)

Festival · saturated network

Reaching the first-aid post when the tower saturates

Each cell tower only accepts a limited number of connections at once. When tens of thousands of festival-goers connect to it, the operator says so itself: calls fail and photos won’t send.

With Siflo: the volunteer team’s messages go from phone to phone, without the tower, all the way to the first-aid post. For the emergency services, call 112.

Source: Bouygues Telecom, “Réseau en festival : pourquoi ça sature” (in French)

Everything you need
to stay in touch.

Your own signals

“Safe”, “Anchor”, “Come”… up to eight signals per group, your club’s way.

The topo in your pocket

The route topo, photos of the approach, the time of the last bus: up to twelve pages, saved on every phone in the group and readable offline. And “Before you go”, a checklist for each activity with its safety sheet. No more headlamps left in the car park.

Distance and direction

Siflo shows how far away and in which direction each member who shares their location is. More reliable than “ten minutes away, just past the ridge”.

On your watch

For those who already look at their watch more often than at the view: at last a useful notification, between the elevation gain and the heart rate. Signal or SOS from your wrist, alerts in the same place. The watch holds no key: everything goes through your phone.

An optional LoRa radio

A small off-the-shelf LoRa radio (Meshtastic-compatible), paired with your phone over Bluetooth: messages, signals and SOS carry 500 m to 2 km with the radio on your belt, and up to 5 km held in hand with a clear line of sight (estimates). A relay radio placed up high extends the chain.

Verified rescuers, with our partners

On a partner’s page, a canyoning site for example, a button creates a Siflo group and offers to invite rescuers whose phones are verified. You choose who to invite, each of them accepts the invitation, nobody joins without your agreement, and everything stays encrypted.

Help

The questions
we get in the car park.

What is the range?

Over Bluetooth, about 25 m from one phone to the next in a pocket, about a hundred metres in hand with a clear line of sight, up to 300 m between two Android phones that support long-range Bluetooth. Every phone in the group then passes the message on to the next, up to 12 hops. With an optional LoRa radio, several kilometres. The details, link by link, are in the Labs.

Do I need a network to use Siflo?

No. Phones talk to each other directly, over Bluetooth and, on Android, nearby Wi-Fi. The internet only helps stitch a scattered group back together: if one phone on each side has signal, messages go through our relay servers.

Does it drain the battery?

Very little: 2% of the battery a day on average, measured on a Google Pixel 10 with Siflo active all day. Transmit power only goes up when a message is not received, and there is a battery saver mode.

Are my messages private?

Yes, they are end-to-end encrypted: only the members of the group, or the person you write to, can read them. Two exceptions, meant to be read by everyone: the nearby channel and the SOS sent to every phone in range. They are signed, not encrypted. It is all in the privacy policy.

Can iPhones and Android phones be in the same group?

Yes, they finally talk to each other, with or without a network. Two differences: long-range Bluetooth and nearby Wi-Fi only exist on Android. A nearby Android shares them with the whole group, iPhones included.

Does Siflo work on my watch?

Yes, with your phone nearby. Apple Watch with an iPhone, from the App Store; Garmin with an Android phone, in the Connect IQ store; Amazfit with an Android phone, in the Zepp app’s store (search for Siflo). Wear OS is coming very soon. Depending on the watch, you can also dictate a reply. Support for Garmin inReach satellite communicators is under study: a request has been sent to the manufacturer.

What is a LoRa radio for?

To reach further than Bluetooth. You need an off-the-shelf Meshtastic-compatible box (Heltec Mesh Node T1, RAK WisMesh Tag…). It carries messages, signals and SOS, but no photos or voice notes. Two members with a radio are enough. Details in the Labs.

What are verified rescuers?

Rescuers and guides whose phones are listed in a register signed by Siflo’s publisher: the app checks it itself, and nobody, not even our relay servers, can slip a name in. They are only offered to you through our partners, when you create a group from their page. You stay in charge: you remove whoever you like, each of them must accept the invitation, and messages stay end-to-end encrypted.

Why not WhatsApp or Signal?

Siflo borrows the best of both: WhatsApp’s simplicity, Signal’s encryption for private messages. But both need the internet and a phone number, and WhatsApp belongs to Meta, which can change its rules: on 8 May 2026, Meta dropped the opt-in end-to-end encryption of Instagram’s private messages (source: BBC). Siflo needs neither.

Is it like BitChat?

Same principle for the nearby network: messages hop from phone to phone, without the internet. Siflo adds private groups run by admins, signals and SOS, long-range Bluetooth, LoRa radios, and one key per private message, as in Signal.

How much does Siflo cost?

Nothing, and it will stay that way. If it helps you out, you can leave a tip from its settings, on Android for now.

Who develops Siflo?

Siflo is developed by Guardia SAS, a French company, under its AI Diligence brand.
